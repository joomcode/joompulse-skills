# Listing optimisation

Diagnose why a listing underperforms and rank the fixes by impact. Two parts: improve the
seller's own listings, then find competitor listings they can beat.

## What to ask for

- **Which listings** — all, one category, or specific IDs.
- **The seller's shop or listing identifier.** The parameter-level comparison needs a
  connected store; without it, fall back to a public comparison and say so.

## Part 1 — Improve the seller's own listings

### Prioritise by revenue, not by how broken something looks

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| High revenue, rating below ~4.5 | **Fix first** — actively losing customers now |
| Low rating, few sales | Fix — the rating is what blocks growth |
| High revenue, high rating | Defend it, and widen the gap |

### Parameter-level gap analysis

Compare the listing against its competitive set parameter by parameter — price, shipping,
fulfilment, reputation, content, media — and sort into three bands:

- **Losing badly** (e.g. no free shipping while nearly all competitors offer it) →
  **mandatory fix**
- **Behind** (e.g. most competitors use fast fulfilment, or have a video) → **scheduled fix**,
  batched by cost
- **Winning** → maintain, and watch the top competitors for threats

Re-run the comparison after each round of changes so the seller can see whether the fix moved
anything.

**Mandatory caveat whenever a comparison score is shown:** it expresses the **percentage of
tracked competitors with a better value**, so **lower means the seller is ahead**. It is
relative to their competitor set, not an absolute grade. Say this every time — a seller who
reads a low number as a bad score will fix the wrong thing.

### Mine the reviews

Summarise the recurring complaints — sizing and fit, packaging damage, durability,
manual language, shipping, service — and turn them into **at least two concrete actions per
listing**. "Reinforce the packaging: about a third of complaints mention damage in transit"
is an action. "Improve quality" is not. Treat this as a repeating cycle, not a one-off.

## Part 2 — Find competitor listings to beat

1. Take the competitor set (see [discover-competitors.md](discover-competitors.md)) and sort
   by revenue — the aim is to learn from a high-selling model.
2. Filter: **exclude official stores**; target **high revenue with a rating below ~4.5**; keep
   sellers whose reputation and standing are **similar to the seller's own**. Do not pick
   fights above their level.
3. Read the complaints on each competitor product and write **at least two concrete actions**
   that would beat it — better packaging, a warranty none of the top sellers offer, a
   Portuguese manual.
4. Work through **around ten** competitor products so patterns emerge rather than anecdotes.

## Output

- **Verdict first:** the single highest-impact fix, and why.
- Per listing: the prioritised fix list, split into mandatory, scheduled and defend, each tied
  to the gap that justifies it.
- The recurring review complaints, with the concrete actions they imply.
- A "products I can clearly beat" list, each with the specific gap to exploit.

Show the comparison-score caveat wherever a score appears. State the estimate disclaimer
once. Show `—` for unavailable values.

## Boundaries

- **The parameter-level comparison needs a connected store.** Ask for the shop or listing
  identifier; if it is not available, do a public comparison instead and say which one was
  used — do not present a public comparison as the connected-store analysis.
- **Title, photo and click-through analysis is not available**, and neither is generating
  titles or descriptions. This diagnoses and prioritises; the seller writes the copy.
- **Never claim a change improved anything without a baseline** to compare against.
- Never present a relative comparison score as an absolute quality grade.
